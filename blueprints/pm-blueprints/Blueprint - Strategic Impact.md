# Blueprint: Strategic Impact

> Domain: Product Strategy | Competency 9 of 12

## What This Competency Means

Strategic Impact is the competency of seeing beyond your product's current boundaries to comprehend macro market dynamics, regulatory landscapes, competitive vectors, and technological paradigm shifts -- and then using that understanding to inform and alter your company's trajectory. It is the difference between a PM who optimizes a feature and a PM who identifies a nascent opportunity that becomes the company's next growth engine.

This competency requires a rare combination of broad pattern recognition and deep conviction. The strategically impactful PM reads widely across industries, understands the forces that create and destroy competitive moats, and can articulate where the market is heading before the data confirms it. They do not just react to competitors; they redefine the competitive frame. They do not just adapt to regulation; they anticipate how regulatory shifts will reshape customer needs.

At its highest expression, strategic impact means being the person in the room who changes the conversation from "How do we improve our current metrics?" to "The entire category is about to be disrupted, and here is how we position ourselves to win." It is the PM equivalent of playing chess while others play checkers -- seeing three moves ahead and having the courage to make the unconventional play.

## Why It Matters in 2026

The convergence of AI, shifting regulatory environments, geopolitical fragmentation, and evolving business models has created an era where strategic dislocation is the norm, not the exception. Paul Adams describes AI as bigger than mobile, possibly as big as the internet itself. Brian Halligan (HubSpot co-founder, Sequoia CEO coach) observes that "starting a company has never been easier, scaling one into a durable high-impact organization has never been harder" -- because the speed of change puts a massive tax on optionality and demands faster, better decision-making from leaders.

In this environment, PMs who only execute on a given roadmap are replaceable by AI agents and junior resources. The irreplaceable PM is the one who can sense a market shift, build the strategic case for a pivot, and rally the organization to move before competitors do. Gibson Biddle's framework of "delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways" becomes even more critical when AI commoditizes the easy-to-copy and compresses margins on the undifferentiated.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

1. **"Delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways"**: Gibson Biddle's DHM framework, learned from Reed Hastings at Netflix, is the most cited product strategy model in Lenny's archive. Hard-to-copy advantages -- brand, unique technology (personalization), original content, network effects, economies of scale -- are the only durable strategic positions. The Netflix DVD experiment showed that even features customers explicitly request may not justify investment when margin impact is analyzed. (*gibson-biddle.md*)

2. **"This is a meteor coming towards you"**: Paul Adams describes AI as a before-and-after moment for product strategy. His diagnostic framework: start with what your product does and why people use it, then ask "Can AI do that?" Map your product against AI capabilities (write, summarize, answer queries, take actions, build rules). For most B2B SaaS products with workflows or multimedia, the meteor is already in their path. (*paul-adams.md*)

3. **"It's never been easier to start a company, and never been harder to scale one"**: Brian Halligan observes that AI-driven speed creates a "massive tax on optionality" -- the ability to move fast and try many things puts pressure on CEOs and product leaders to be faster and better decision-makers. His LOCK algorithm for evaluating strategic leaders: Lovable (inspires followership), Obsessed (deep founder-market fit), Chip on shoulder (driven by constructive dissatisfaction), Knowledgeable (deep domain expertise). (*brian-halligan.md*)

4. **Shopify's strategic coherence through Conway's Law**: Shopify moved from 10 GM-led business units to two divisions (Core and Merchant Services) specifically because strategic impact requires product coherence. Glen Coates: "The best companies figure out how to make sure you can't see the org chart through the product." Their founder-led culture, where Tobi's yearly themes cascade into every team's priorities, ensures strategic alignment from the top through to execution. (*how-shopify-builds-product.md*)

5. **Elena Verna on why growth teams cannot fix strategic problems**: Elena Verna warns that when a business is declining, hiring a head of growth is not the answer -- "growth can amplify great product market fit" but cannot fix degrading PMF or being "eaten by a competitor stomping on your territory." The strategic diagnosis must come first: is the core product and go-to-market strategy sound? Only then can growth efforts amplify results. (*elena-verna-30.md*)

6. **Obsessing over competition is counterproductive; understanding competition is essential**: Elena Verna draws a sharp line: know what competitors are doing, study their flows and monetization strategies, be inspired by aspects of their approach -- but never blatantly copy. "Every single experience is very unique to their customer, to their channel. You don't even know if you're getting their actual experience." Copying fails 95% of the time. Strategic impact comes from understanding competitive dynamics, not mimicking tactics. (*elena-verna-30.md*)

7. **The brain's threshold for strategic thinking**: Evan LaPointe explains that ideas sort into believed, believable, conceivable, and inconceivable categories in our brains. Strategic vision often lives in the "conceivable but unbelievable" zone, which triggers resistance in people low on openness (a Big Five personality trait). Understanding this cognitive dynamic is essential for PMs who need to sell unconventional strategic ideas to skeptical executives. (*evan-lapointe.md*)

8. **Intercom's "bet the farm" strategic pivot**: When Sam Altman said customer service would be one of the first industries disrupted by AI, Intercom already had a machine learning team -- but they had to completely rethink their product from the ground up. The result, Fin, can now answer 50-70% of inbound customer queries for some customers. Strategic impact meant recognizing the disruption, making the hard call to abandon existing plans, and shipping decisively. (*paul-adams.md*)

## How to Build This Competency

**Frameworks to adopt:**
- **DHM Strategy Model (Gibson Biddle)**: For every major initiative, evaluate whether it delights customers, builds hard-to-copy advantage, and enhances margins. If it only achieves one, it is tactically useful but strategically insufficient.
- **AI Disruption Map (Paul Adams)**: List everything your product does. For each capability, assess: can AI do this? Will it be able to soon? Is this replacement or augmentation? Use this to identify which parts of your product are defensible and which are in the meteor's path.
- **Competitive Intelligence Practice (Elena Verna)**: Create fake accounts across competitor products quarterly. Map their onboarding, monetization, activation, and retention flows. Identify patterns and inspiration, but never copy -- instead, ask "What can we learn about what the market values?"
- **Believed-to-Inconceivable Spectrum (Evan LaPointe)**: When presenting strategic ideas, assess where they fall on the believability spectrum for your audience. Pre-build the bridge from believed to believable to conceivable before asking anyone to act on something they find inconceivable.

**Practices:**
- Read outside your industry weekly. The best strategic insights come from adjacent and unrelated domains.
- Attend (or listen to) your company's board meetings, earnings calls, or investor updates. Understand the language and concerns of the people who fund your company.
- Build a competitive landscape map that you update quarterly. Include not just direct competitors but adjacent products, potential platform shifts, and regulatory changes.
- Write a "pre-mortem" for your product once a year: "It is 2028 and our product has failed. What happened?" This forces you to confront strategic risks you are ignoring.
- Cultivate a personal board of directors (a la Gibson Biddle) -- three to five people across different domains who can challenge your strategic thinking and expose your blind spots.

## Self-Assessment Questions

1. Can you name the three biggest threats to your product's market position over the next three years, and do you have a plan for each?
2. When was the last time you changed your team's direction based on a market insight you surfaced -- not one that was handed to you by leadership?
3. Could you explain your product's competitive moat to a board member in under two minutes, including what makes it hard to copy?
4. Have you mapped your product's capabilities against what AI can now do? Do you know which parts of your product are in the path of disruption?
5. Do you regularly read and synthesize information from outside your immediate industry, and has that ever influenced a strategic decision?
