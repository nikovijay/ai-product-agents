# Blueprint: Product Vision & Roadmapping

> Domain: Product Strategy | Competency 8 of 12

## What This Competency Means

Product Vision & Roadmapping is the craft of defining an inspiring multi-year trajectory for your product and then translating that trajectory into a ruthlessly prioritized sequence of bets. Vision answers "Where are we going and why does it matter?" Roadmapping answers "What do we build next, and in what order, to unlock the most value along the way?"

This competency is not about producing a Gantt chart or a feature list on a timeline. It is about creating a narrative so compelling that engineers, designers, executives, and customers can see themselves in a future that does not yet exist -- and then making hard sequencing decisions that sacrifice good ideas in service of great ones. The best product visions are not detailed blueprints; they are painted pictures of a world transformed by the product's existence, specific enough to guide decisions but flexible enough to absorb new information.

Roadmapping, in turn, is the practice of disciplined saying-no. Every feature on a roadmap represents dozens of features that did not make the cut. The PM who excels here understands that the sequence in which you build things matters as much as what you build. A feature shipped too early wastes resources; a feature shipped too late concedes the market. The roadmap is a living argument about where the highest-leverage work lies right now.

## Why It Matters in 2026

The pace of change in 2026 demands that product teams hold vision and adaptability in tension simultaneously. AI is reshaping entire product categories in months, not years. As Paul Adams (Intercom CPO) described, the launch of ChatGPT was a "before and after moment" that caused Intercom to rip up their strategy and rebuild from first principles overnight. Teams that cling to rigid roadmaps will be disrupted; teams without a vision will chase every shiny object.

The companies winning in 2026 -- Shopify with CEO-set yearly themes, Miro with their three-year painted picture, Stripe with real product strategies that make planning take three days instead of six weeks -- all share a pattern: strong directional vision paired with lightweight, adaptable planning processes. The annual planning ritual that consumes six weeks and is abandoned by March is a relic. The future belongs to PMs who can articulate a clear strategy and let that strategy do the heavy lifting of prioritization.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

1. **"If you have a real product strategy, planning should be a breeze"**: Shreyas Doshi describes how having an authentic, pre-aligned product strategy for Stripe Connect reduced his annual planning from six weeks to three days. The insight: most planning pain comes from a lack of strategy, not a lack of process. "Plans are useless, but planning is everything" is a cop-out -- a real strategy eliminates the need for the planning theater. (*shreyas-doshi-live.md*)

2. **Shopify's yearly themes over OKRs**: Shopify CEO Tobi Lutke sets broad themes annually, written from the merchant's perspective (e.g., "Shopify keeps me on the cutting edge"). These themes become a focusing lens that allows teams to self-organize around six-month roadmaps with four six-week cycles. Glen Coates explains Shopify's aversion to OKRs: they lead to "micro-optimizations of local maxima" where the product "doesn't feel good anymore." (*how-shopify-builds-product.md*)

3. **Miro's "three-year painted picture"**: Miro's leadership co-develops a bold, ambitious three-year painted picture that establishes directional outcomes for users and the company. This informs an annual strategy built collaboratively over three months, resulting in a rolling six-month roadmap with 80% confidence in the current quarter and 50%+ in the next. The key learning: they reduced OKR layers, moved to semi-annual frequency, and limited objectives to three or four with two to three KRs each. (*how-miro-builds-product.md*)

4. **The art of saying no -- five response paths**: Lenny's framework for saying no effectively provides five calibrated responses ranging from "Yes, but here's what will need to change" to "No, because it's a bad idea for reasons XYZ." The critical skill is forming your own independent point of view before responding, and evaluating ideas against strategy, opportunity cost, and potential upside. (*saying-no.md*)

5. **Prioritizing at pre-PMF startups -- "Make 10 customers very happy"**: Lenny's SUSS framework (Segment, Understand, Solve, Stay focused) argues that the single goal of early-stage prioritization is making 10 customers very happy. Allocate ~80% of effort to increasing product value, ~10% to onboarding, ~10% to delight. The biggest pitfall: trying to be data-driven when you simply do not have enough data. (*prioritizing-at-startups.md*)

6. **Vision as a PM career ladder skill**: Lenny's analysis of 20+ PM career ladders found that only 40% of companies list vision as an essential PM skill -- surprisingly low -- but those that do describe it as: "Crafts a broad, long-term vision for the product area" and "Articulates a vision that inspires, resonates, and drives clarity." The progression: junior PMs contribute to a roadmap given to them; senior PMs own the roadmap; leaders review roadmaps. (*skills-pms-need-to-build.md*)

7. **Mission to Vision to Strategy to Goals to Roadmap**: Lenny's goal-setting framework establishes the full cascade. Using Tesla as an example: Mission (accelerate transition to sustainable energy) informs Strategy (build sports car, then affordable car) informs Goals (production targets). The "silver burrito" metaphor: without explicit goals, team members imagine different outcomes inside the same project. (*setting-goals---issue-23.md*)

8. **Paul Adams on ripping up strategy for AI**: Intercom's CPO describes how they responded to ChatGPT by going back to first principles: "What is my product for? Why do people love it? Can AI do that?" This led to rebuilding their entire strategy around AI-first customer support. The lesson: vision must be grounded in enduring customer problems, but roadmaps must be ruthlessly adaptable to paradigm shifts. (*paul-adams.md*)

## How to Build This Competency

**Frameworks to adopt:**
- **Painted Picture (Miro model)**: Write a three-year narrative of what the world looks like for your users when your product fulfills its promise. Make it bold and specific enough to guide decisions, but not so detailed that it becomes a feature list.
- **Theme-Based Planning (Shopify model)**: Set three to six broad themes per year, written from the customer's perspective. Let teams self-organize around these themes with six-month roadmaps and six-week sprints.
- **Strategy-First Planning (Shreyas Doshi model)**: Invest heavily in a clear product strategy document that has pre-alignment from all stakeholders. When planning season arrives, the strategy does the work -- planning becomes a translation exercise, not a negotiation.
- **The Five Paths of Saying No (Lenny model)**: Practice all five responses. Build the muscle of evaluating every incoming idea against your strategy, forming an independent view, and responding constructively.

**Practices:**
- Write your product vision as a narrative, not a slide deck. Describe what a user's day looks like in three years because your product exists. Share it widely and update it annually.
- Review your roadmap monthly against your vision. For each item, ask: "Does this move us meaningfully toward the painted picture?" If not, it should be challenged.
- Limit your roadmap to no more than three major bets per quarter. If everything is a priority, nothing is.
- Practice the discipline of sequencing: ask "What must be true before this feature can succeed?" to identify the right build order.
- When a new opportunity arises (AI shift, competitor move, executive request), run it through Lenny's five-path framework before reacting.

## Self-Assessment Questions

1. Can you articulate your product's three-year vision in two minutes, in a way that would inspire a new hire on their first day?
2. Does your roadmap clearly reflect a sequencing logic -- could you explain why item A must come before item B?
3. When was the last time you said no to a stakeholder request, and did you offer a constructive alternative?
4. If you have a product strategy document, is it actively used for decision-making, or does it sit in a folder unread since planning season?
5. Could your team independently make prioritization decisions that align with your vision, without consulting you on each one?
