# Blueprint: Voice of the Customer (VoC)

> Domain: Customer Insight | Competency 5 of 12

## What This Competency Means

Voice of the Customer is the systematic practice of aggregating, interpreting, and acting on user feedback across every channel available -- usability studies, Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) interviews, sentiment analysis, support tickets, community forums, and continuous discovery conversations. A PM with strong VoC competency does not merely collect feedback reactively; they build systems that ensure their team is in constant contact with the reality of their users' lives.

This competency requires mastery of multiple research methods and knowing when to deploy each. JTBD interviews reveal the causal forces behind switching behavior. Continuous discovery habits ensure you are talking to customers every week, not just before major launches. Usability testing catches friction before it reaches production. Sentiment analysis at scale surfaces patterns that individual conversations miss. The VoC-fluent PM synthesizes all of these signals into a coherent understanding of what customers need, want, and struggle with -- even when customers cannot articulate it themselves.

At its deepest level, VoC is about developing what Christian Idiodi of SVPG calls the "competence that earns trust" -- knowing your customers so well that the organization trusts you to make decisions on their behalf. It is the foundation upon which all other PM competencies rest, because without genuine understanding of your users, every strategy, every prioritization, and every design decision is built on assumptions.

## Why It Matters in 2026

In 2026, the paradox of customer insight has intensified. On one hand, AI tools can now synthesize thousands of support tickets, interview transcripts, and survey responses in minutes, making it easier than ever to process feedback at scale. On the other hand, this very capability creates a dangerous temptation to substitute automated summarization for genuine human understanding. The PM who relies solely on AI-generated insights without ever sitting across from a customer will miss the context, emotion, and nuance that drive real product breakthroughs.

Meanwhile, as Gustaf Alstromer of Y Combinator bluntly states: "If I drill down what makes companies fail, it's quite simple. They don't talk to users, which means they don't find product-market fit. And if they don't find product-market fit, nothing else really matters." This remains as true for Series D companies launching new product lines as it is for pre-seed startups. The companies winning in 2026 -- from Nubank's fanatical customer love to Notion's user-driven product development -- are the ones that have institutionalized customer closeness, not as a periodic research project but as a continuous operating discipline.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

1. **Teresa Torres: "98% of people that write opportunities write them as solutions."** Teresa Torres, author of Continuous Discovery Habits, explained that the heart of good product is staying in the problem space before jumping to solutions. She recommends structuring the opportunity space using experience maps -- mapping distinct moments in the user journey and capturing the needs, pain points, and desires that arise at each stage. "Whether I work at Netflix or Hulu, our opportunity spaces probably look pretty similar. Now the ones we choose to go after and how we solve them might look really different."
   *Source: teresa-torres.md (podcast)*

2. **Teresa Torres on interviewing: "We're not very good at answering direct questions out of context."** She argues that most product teams ask customers direct questions like "What do you like about X?" -- but these produce unreliable answers because our brains generate fast, decontextualized responses that don't reflect actual behavior. Instead, she advocates collecting stories: "Opportunities emerge from our customers' stories." Story-based interviewing captures the context and nuance that direct questioning misses.
   *Source: teresa-torres.md (podcast)*

3. **Bob Moesta on JTBD: "It's not pain and gain, it's context and outcome."** The co-creator of the Jobs to Be Done framework explained that the biggest misconception about JTBD is reducing it to pain points. The real power comes from understanding the full context that makes seemingly irrational behavior rational: "The moment you hear a story and it seems irrational, nine times out of 10, it's because you don't have the rest of the story." He uses the four forces model -- Push (F1), Pull (F2), Anxiety of the New (F3), and Habit of the Present (F4) -- to map the system of behavior that determines whether someone will switch products.
   *Source: bob-moesta.md (podcast)*

4. **Bob Moesta: "What people say they want and what they actually do are different things."** He shared that 93% of surveyed homebuyers said they wanted an Energy Star compliant house ($30K extra), but when it came time to buy, nobody chose it -- they all chose the finished basement instead. His method is based not on traditional market research but on "criminal and intelligence interrogation" -- getting people to tell the story of how they made a specific decision, uncovering the real trade-offs.
   *Source: bob-moesta.md (podcast)*

5. **Christian Idiodi (SVPG): The single most important discovery method is working with reference customers.** When asked to pick one approach to figure out what to build out of all the frameworks available (press releases, story mapping, opportunity solution trees, fake door tests), he recommended finding reference customers to work with closely. He also emphasized that the core PM competency is being "the person in the organization who represents the customer the best, who has deep knowledge of the customers and users, the data, the industry, and the business."
   *Source: christian-idiodi.md (podcast)*

6. **Gustaf Alstromer (YC): "YC's headline is 'Make things people want' and it's still true."** After working with 600+ startups, he identified the primary cause of failure as simply not talking to users. He noted that the best founders recreate the YC ethos inside their companies: "doing things that don't scale and talking to customers." At YC Office Hours, the first question is always: "What's holding you back from moving faster?" -- and the answer almost always connects to insufficient customer understanding.
   *Source: gustaf-alstromer.md (podcast)*

7. **Jag Duggal (Nubank): "We rarely scale a project until we know the Sean Ellis score hit a threshold."** Nubank, with 80-90% word-of-mouth growth, operationalizes customer love by measuring it rigorously before scaling. Their discovery process focuses "not simply on asking the customer, but on innovating on their behalf... observe them and find their pain points even when they aren't noticing it." The company's first value -- "We want our customers to love us fanatically" -- is not aspirational; it is a measurable decision criterion.
   *Source: jag-duggal.md (podcast)*

8. **Kristen Berman (Irrational Labs): "Don't listen to your customers -- watch what they do."** Behavioral science teaches that people act against their stated preferences. A popular FinTech app's most requested feature was budgeting. After building it and testing with 10,000 users: zero change in average spend, zero change in behavior. The insight: mapping every step of the target behavior ("behavioral diagnosis") would have predicted this failure, because budgeting requires too many cognitive steps to actually change spending behavior.
   *Source: kristen-berman.md (podcast)*

## How to Build This Competency

**Establish a continuous discovery cadence:**
- Commit to talking to at least one customer per week. Teresa Torres' minimum viable cadence is weekly touchpoints with customers -- not monthly, not quarterly.
- Automate the recruiting process. Use in-app prompts, a standing Calendly link, or a customer advisory panel to reduce the friction of scheduling interviews.
- Use the Opportunity Solution Tree to structure what you learn: Outcome at the root, opportunities (needs/pain points/desires) branching off experience map stages, solutions branching from opportunities.

**Master story-based interviewing:**
- Never ask "What do you want?" or "Would you use X?" Instead, ask "Tell me about the last time you [did the relevant behavior]." Follow the timeline backwards and forwards to uncover context.
- Use Bob Moesta's four forces framework in every interview: What pushed them away from the old way? What pulled them toward the new? What anxieties held them back? What habits kept them stuck?
- Interview people who recently made a decision (switched products, signed up, churned) -- not people who "might" do something in the future.

**Build organizational VoC infrastructure:**
- Create a shared repository of customer insights (not just raw transcripts) that the entire product team can access. Tag insights by opportunity area.
- Establish a "customer insight of the week" ritual where team members share one surprising finding from a recent conversation.
- Synthesize quantitative signals (support ticket themes, NPS verbatims, app store reviews, social sentiment) alongside qualitative interviews. Neither alone is sufficient.

**Avoid common pitfalls:**
- Do not confuse feature requests with underlying needs. A request for "export to CSV" may actually be a need for "share data with my finance team."
- Do not over-index on power users. They are the loudest but may not represent the adjacent users who represent your growth opportunity.
- Do not substitute surveys for conversations. Surveys tell you what; conversations tell you why.

## Self-Assessment Questions

1. How many customers did you personally speak with in the last 30 days? If the answer is fewer than four, what structural barriers are preventing weekly customer contact, and how can you remove them?

2. Can you draw your product's opportunity space as a tree, with the user experience mapped across distinct moments, and needs/pain points identified at each stage -- without referencing feature names or solutions?

3. When was the last time a customer conversation changed your team's priorities or killed a feature that was on the roadmap? If you cannot recall one, is your research actually influencing decisions?

4. Do you interview customers who recently churned or switched to a competitor with the same rigor as you interview satisfied users? What have you learned from the "firing" stories?

5. Can you articulate the difference between what your customers say they want and what their behavior reveals they actually need? What is the most striking gap you have identified between stated preference and observed behavior?
