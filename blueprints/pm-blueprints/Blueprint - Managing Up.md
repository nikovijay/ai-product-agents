# Blueprint: Managing Up

> Domain: Influencing People | Competency 12 of 12

## What This Competency Means

Managing up is the discipline of interfacing effectively with senior executive leadership -- your direct manager, their manager, and the broader executive team. It encompasses abstracting complex technical details and granular metrics into strategic narratives that executives can act on. It includes securing budget and headcount by framing resource requests in terms of business outcomes. It involves managing executive exposure to risk by delivering bad news early, clearly, and with a path forward. And it means building the kind of trust that earns you increasing autonomy over time.

Managing up is often misunderstood as political maneuvering or impression management. In reality, it is about creating a productive working relationship with the people who control your team's resources, set your constraints, and evaluate your performance. The best practitioners of this skill treat their manager as a partner, not a gatekeeper. They proactively provide the context their leaders need, translate between the detail level their team operates at and the altitude their executives think at, and make it as easy as possible for their boss to champion them in rooms they are not in.

This competency also includes the art of strategic storytelling: the ability to take a complex product situation -- competing priorities, uncertain data, technical constraints, market shifts -- and distill it into a narrative that is clear, compelling, and actionable for a senior leader who has three minutes of attention and a dozen other problems on their plate.

## Why It Matters in 2026

In 2026, the relationship between PMs and executives has intensified on multiple fronts. Companies are running leaner, which means resource allocation decisions are more competitive and more scrutinized. AI is changing what is possible, which means executives are constantly asking "Why aren't we moving faster?" and "What should we be doing with AI?" PMs who cannot translate technical possibilities and constraints into strategic language will be left out of the conversations that determine their team's future.

The rise of the founder-mode ethos has also created a dynamic where many CEOs are more deeply involved in product decisions than they were during the era of professional management. PMs must learn to work productively with intensely product-minded executives who have strong opinions and want to be involved in the details. This requires knowing when to involve leadership, how to present options rather than decisions, and how to maintain creative autonomy for the team while keeping the executive informed and invested.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

1. **Know what you want and make it easy for your boss to get you there.** Claire Vo describes her approach to career advancement: "Know what you want out of your career, be clear and ask for it, and then make it easy for your boss or whoever can support or champion you to get you from here to there." She walked into her boss's office with an org chart, her name at the top, a management structure proposal, and a job description already written. (Source: `claire-vo.md`)

2. **Frame promotion asks as solving company problems, not personal career goals.** Claire Vo: "The conversation needs to be about what you being in a different position does for the company and why the company needs it. Not: I want to be promoted because I want to be a director." Focus on why a role is necessary for the organization and why you are the best fit, not on your desire for career progression. (Source: `claire-vo.md`)

3. **Use the autonomy template to build trust with your manager.** Jackie Bavaro recommends a three-part structure for manager updates: (1) "Let me tell you about a challenge I ran into," (2) "Here is how I am handling it," (3) "Any thoughts?" This simultaneously demonstrates competence, shares relevant context, and opens the door for feedback -- building trust that earns you more autonomy over time. (Source: `becoming-a-senior-product-manager.md`)

4. **Executives have "dinosaur brains" -- bring the recommendation, not the data dump.** Ami Vora, CPO at Faire, tells her teams: "Assume that executives have a little tiny dinosaur brain. We can really only hold three facts at the same time." The best service you can do is make a clear recommendation rather than presenting all the information for the executive to process. "My manager owns context, I own the recommendation." (Source: `ami-vora.md`)

5. **Use product reviews to calibrate on principles, not just get decisions.** Vora argues that product reviews should produce principles about how to make decisions in the future, not just answers to today's questions. "You always want to change the org to constantly make better decisions." The goal is to build decision-making capacity across the organization so that fewer decisions need to escalate. (Source: `ami-vora.md`)

6. **Find an influential champion for calibration processes.** Lenny describes the most underappreciated ingredient in getting promoted: "Having someone with influence fight for you in the calibration process." Give your champion ammunition by hitting goals, keeping them updated on how you have addressed gaps, and maintaining a wins document. "Treat your manager as a partner, not a gatekeeper." (Source: `how-to-get-promoted.md`)

7. **Manage the clock in executive meetings.** Jules Walter: "With senior executives, if you don't exit the meeting with a decision, you sometimes might not be able to get ahold of them again for weeks or months." Start every meeting with an explicit desired outcome, use concise language as if speaking to someone with no context, and redirect tangential conversations firmly: "Since we only have 5 minutes left, let's jump to the decision we need today." (Source: `a-pms-guide-to-influence.md`)

8. **The U-curve model for involving founder-CEOs.** Noah Weiss describes the optimal pattern for involving product-minded executives: high involvement at the start (strategic alignment, goals, anti-goals), low involvement in the middle (let the team explore), and high involvement at the end (quality review, "taste the soup"). This gives the team creative space while ensuring the executive feels ownership. (Source: `noah-weiss.md`)

## How to Build This Competency

**Master the Art of Strategic Translation**
- Practice the "elevator pitch" exercise: take your current project's status and distill it into three sentences that a board member would understand. Lead with impact, not activity. "We shipped a new onboarding flow" becomes "We increased activation by 15%, which adds $2M in annual recurring revenue."
- Use the Minto Pyramid Principle: start with the conclusion, then support it. Executives want to know the answer first and the reasoning second.
- Develop fluency in financial language. Learn to express product outcomes in terms of revenue, margin, customer lifetime value, and market share -- the language your CFO and CEO think in.

**Build a Proactive Communication Rhythm**
- Establish a regular cadence of written updates to your manager. A brief weekly email covering wins, risks, decisions needed, and what is coming next eliminates surprises and builds trust.
- Use the autonomy template (challenge, approach, request for feedback) in every 1:1. Over time, this builds a track record that earns you more latitude.
- When something goes wrong, report it before it is discovered. Frame bad news with three components: what happened, what the impact is, and what you are doing about it.

**Present with Executive Precision**
- For any executive meeting, state the desired outcome in the first 30 seconds. "Our goal today is to get approval on the Q3 roadmap" gives you legitimacy to redirect tangential discussions.
- Limit your presentation to three key points. If an executive needs to remember more than three things from your meeting, you have not done your job.
- Prepare for questions by studying each executive's patterns: What do they always ask about? What are they afraid of? What metrics do they track? Build answers to their predictable questions into your presentation.

**Secure Resources Through Business Cases**
- Frame every resource request in terms of outcomes, not inputs. Not "I need two more engineers" but "With two additional engineers, we can launch the enterprise tier three months earlier, capturing an estimated $5M in pipeline."
- Build allies before budget conversations. Ensure that the people who influence resource allocation have already heard your pitch and understand its business value.
- Maintain a portfolio of quick wins alongside your big bets so that your team's track record of impact is always fresh when allocation decisions are made.

**Manage Executive Expectations on Risk**
- Never hide risks. Surface them early with a clear assessment of probability, impact, and mitigation plan.
- Use the "traffic light" model: green (on track), yellow (risk identified, mitigation in progress), red (blocked, need help). Update this weekly.
- When presenting tradeoffs, steelman the alternatives. Show that you have considered the strongest version of the path you are not recommending.

## Self-Assessment Questions

1. If your manager were asked in a calibration meeting to describe your top three contributions this quarter, could they do it accurately and compellingly? Have you given them the information they need to champion you?

2. When was the last time you delivered bad news to an executive? Did you report it proactively or reactively? Did you include a mitigation plan, or just the problem?

3. Think about your last executive presentation. Could you rewrite it to be half the length and twice as clear? What would you cut, and what would you keep?

4. Do you know what your manager's top three priorities are this quarter and what keeps them up at night? If not, when will you ask?

5. How often do you communicate with your manager through written updates versus waiting for scheduled 1:1s? Could a proactive communication rhythm give you more autonomy and reduce the number of surprise questions you face?
