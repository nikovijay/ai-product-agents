# How Ramp Builds Product

> Company Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## Company Overview

Ramp provides corporate cards and spend management, helping companies save time and money. Valued at over $8 billion, Ramp is arguably the fastest-growing SaaS company of all time, hitting $100 million ARR in just two years and growing 4x to over $10 billion in annualized spending. What makes Ramp's product org remarkable is its obsessive culture of velocity: fewer than 5 PMs and 50 engineers shipped the product to $100M ARR. Under VP of Product Geoff Charles, every process, org decision, and hiring choice is optimized for speed of execution.

## Org Structure

- **PM, Design, Engineering, and Data all report to the CTO.** Having one tech-minded leader over all functions forces cooperation and prevents Product from becoming a "stakeholder" to Engineering rather than a "co-founder."
- **14 PMs** at time of profiling (~500+ employees). PM hiring is deliberately restricted until product is the obvious bottleneck to velocity.
- **Teams organized around business outcomes.** Each pod owns an accountable, measurable business goal (e.g., drive 50% of sales qualified leads via automated outbound, build a Bill.com competitor in three months).
- **Each pod has embedded cross-functional support:** dedicated Product Marketer, Product Operator, Product Partnerships Lead, and Product Counsel mapped to the tech team.
- **Small teams (5-10 engineers max)** that can split into sub-pods as they grow. Pods spin out once they have proven wins and need to work across teams.
- **Flexible staffing regardless of reporting lines.** People move between teams based on business needs. Management (hiring, coaching, development) stays stable while pod composition shifts.
- **Platform teams stay embedded** in core product teams as long as possible to maintain business context. They spin out only after multiple wins prove they need to serve multiple teams.

## Planning Process

- **Planning evolved with company stage:**
  - Pre-PMF (<20 people): Two-week sprints, no backlog, laser focus on shipping one thing at a time.
  - Post-PMF (100+ people): Monthly-to-quarterly planning for GTM coordination and system durability.
  - Multi-product (500+ people): Quarterly roadmaps with bi-annual company-level OKRs.
- **Bottom-up product strategy:** Pod-level roadmaps are developed bottom-up by the people closest to the problem. Leadership aligns on vision and top 2-3 company goals, then empowers teams.
- **Product strategy framework (7 elements):**
  1. Goal -- What do you want to see in the world?
  2. Hypothesis -- Why do you think this will work?
  3. Right to Win -- Why are we uniquely positioned?
  4. Metric -- How will you measure it?
  5. Initiatives -- What do we need to do?
  6. Risks -- Why would we fail and what do we do about it?
  7. Long Term Outcomes -- How will this compound?
- **Strategy anchored to the financial model.** Product strategy that is not tied to how the company makes money will not survive. Each quarter may emphasize different financial levers (margins, credit losses, new revenue streams).
- **OKRs used sparingly at company level only.** Limited to a few large cross-functional projects or launches. OKRs are explicitly not used for performance management. "If we don't hit the goal, that does not necessarily mean everyone tied to the goal underperformed."
- **No backlog.** If a bug matters, fix it now (on-call engineers handle this). If it is not fixed, it will escalate again if important. Backlog grooming is considered low-leverage in a high-velocity environment.

## How They Execute

- **Build in the open.** Every spec, design, decision, and status update is published in project-specific Slack channels. Anyone can read and opine. Teams farm for dissent, not approval.
- **Weekly product "jam session"** for key decisions and high-risk projects. PMs decide what needs input. Product and design leadership block one hour every Wednesday at 4pm. Teams sign up. Engineering leaders attend; non-tech stakeholders are excluded (their input should be in the spec already).
- **Design principles for reviews:**
  1. Focus only on new products or major UX changes with big potential impact.
  2. Lightweight, frequent, non-blocking.
  3. Product and design leadership aligned in the same room.
  4. Relevant technical leaders included; others excluded.
- **#UX-input Slack channel:** Anyone at the company can post UX improvements. Posts are triaged via emoji, auto-creating Linear tickets routed to the right teams. GPT summarizes issues. Teams burn down a fixed percentage each sprint.
- **No burndown charts, velocity tracking, or story points.** "We don't track tasks -- we track whether you shipped what you said you were going to ship."
- **Tooling is team's choice.** Some use notepads, some use GitHub, some use Linear. The only enforced standard is high clarity and high succinctness in published communication.

## What Makes Them Distinctive

1. **Velocity as the core cultural value.** Speed is treated as a competitive advantage in its own right. "If your speed of shipping is extremely high, the cost of being wrong is much lower." Every process, org design choice, and hiring decision is evaluated through the lens of velocity.
2. **PMs sell the first 5-10 customers.** Product managers must find, pitch, and close the first customers for their products. If you cannot find 10 customers, you have not built something people want, and GTM teams will not be engaged.
3. **Hire for slope, not intercept.** Ramp greatly discounts domain expertise in favor of learning ability and intrinsic curiosity. They hire former (and future) founders. The three PMs who left Ramp all started companies.
4. **4-step headcount control process:** (1) Does this accelerate OKRs? (2) Can automation do it? (3) Can someone existing flex into it? (4) Only then, hire -- and hold the manager accountable for defined impact.
5. **#gratitude Slack channel ("couscous"):** Anyone can publicly thank colleagues, and gratitude is expressed at the start of every sprint. Named after a team in Morocco that helped bootstrap early platform work.

## What You Can Steal

1. **Use the 7-element product strategy framework.** For every initiative, write down: Goal, Hypothesis, Right to Win, Metric, Initiatives, Risks, and Long Term Outcomes. It forces rigor without the overhead of traditional OKRs.
2. **Have PMs sell the first 5-10 customers.** Before engaging GTM teams, make the PM responsible for finding, pitching, and closing early customers. It validates demand and forces deep customer empathy.
3. **Farm for dissent, not approval.** Publish specs, designs, and decisions in open channels. Anyone can opine. Remove gatekeepers so teams keep moving while still getting diverse input.
4. **Run a 4-step headcount test before hiring.** Ask: Does this accelerate our goals? Can automation handle it? Can someone existing do it? Only hire if all three answers are no.
5. **Anchor product strategy to the financial model.** Ensure every product roadmap explicitly connects to how the company makes money. Align with finance each quarter on which financial levers matter most.

## Recommended Books

| Book | Author | Why It's Relevant |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| Amp It Up | Frank Slootman | The defining playbook for Ramp's velocity-first culture, showing how to raise execution intensity, eliminate waste, and compress timelines across an entire organisation. |
| High Output Management | Andy Grove | Provides the intellectual framework for Ramp's output-over-process philosophy, where shipping results matters more than tracking story points. |
| The Lean Startup | Eric Ries | Reinforces Ramp's approach of validating ideas fast, having PMs sell the first 5-10 customers, and treating speed as a competitive advantage that lowers the cost of being wrong. |
| Good Strategy Bad Strategy | Richard Rumelt | Essential for understanding Ramp's 7-element product strategy framework, which demands a clear hypothesis, right to win, and long-term compounding logic for every initiative. |
