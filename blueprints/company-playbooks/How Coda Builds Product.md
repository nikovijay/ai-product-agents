# How Coda Builds Product

> Company Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## Company Overview

Coda is an all-in-one doc platform that combines documents, spreadsheets, and applications into a single surface. Founded by Shishir Mehrotra (former VP of Product & Engineering at YouTube/Google, Spotify board member), the company has built one of the most deliberate, first-principled product cultures in tech. With approximately 15 PMs and a joint Product and Design organization led by CPO Lane Shackleton, Coda is known for its obsessive approach to team rituals, its "Right vs. Familiar" value system, and its practice of dogfooding their own product for virtually every internal workflow.

## Org Structure

Coda operates a joint Product and Design team -- a deliberate choice born from Lane's observation at Google/YouTube that PMs became de facto decision-makers not through intent but because they accumulated the most context. By unifying the teams, PMs and designers share the same context and information, leading to more fluid roles. Sometimes designers write specs; sometimes PMs draw in Figma.

Structure: PMs report to PM leads, and both PM and Design leads report to CPO Lane Shackleton, who reports to CEO Shishir Mehrotra. Teams are structured around outcomes (e.g., a team focused on driving revenue mixing PDE and Customer roles). Within broader teams, "Investment Areas" (IAs) focus on specific journeys like onboarding or long-term outcomes like ecosystem growth.

Small teams with autonomy are the operating unit: roughly 1 PM, 1 designer, and 2-4 engineers, sometimes with Data and Insights support. The team composition is roughly 15 PMs and 30+ engineers. Team structure iterates about once per year, but core elements have remained consistent.

## Planning Process

Coda's planning has evolved through three distinct phases as the company grew:

**Phase 1 (Early, 2-3 PMs, 10-15 engineers):** Two-week sprints with daily standups. Every six weeks (three sprints), a check-in for reflection and quick re-planning.

**Phase 2 (~30 engineers, 4-5 PMs):** Six-week sprints coupled with H1/H2 (half-yearly) planning. The longer sprints created space for strategy work while maintaining execution velocity.

**Phase 3 (Current, 12-15 PMs): "Quarterly Plus" planning** tied to annual Big Bets. The process:

1. **Annual Big Bets:** Inspired by *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*, the team identifies the most important challenges facing the business. Leaders enumerate and rank key challenges, develop strategies against them, run $100 voting exercises for alignment, debate, and decide on a small set of Big Bets.

2. **Quarterly Plus:** Teams plan concrete commitments for the current quarter plus a look-ahead to the next quarter. This acknowledges that six-month plans are unreliable but directional context is valuable. The artifact is a two-page writeup with Dory (questions) and Pulse (sentiment gathering).

A core planning rule: planning should take no more than 10% of the execution time period (e.g., less than 9 days for a quarter).

**OKRs:** Coda uses OKRs but makes it clear they are not a strategy replacement. They reject the "70% = success" model (calling it "a luxury of having a money-printing machine in your basement") and instead goal around 100% of commitments. Teams distinguish between "input" metrics (things they control) and "output" metrics (lagging indicators like activation). OKR writeups include the why behind the goals, embed OKR tables within narrative context, and include a Dory for async Q&A.

## How They Execute

Coda has two core review forums:

**Catalyst:** An hour-long block, three times per week at 1 PM PT. Anyone can sign up. Clear roles for each session:
- **Driver:** Runs the meeting, facilitates decisions (often the PM)
- **Makers:** The doers who will execute
- **Braintrust:** Guides and provides context (often leaders)
- **Interested:** Observers who want to follow along

An automated Slack reminder goes out the day before with the topic, links to review, and role expectations. Key reminder for reviewers: "Add lift, not drag."

**Design Huddle:** Weekly forum where designers bring work at any stage for feedback. Round-robin style, 2-4 topics per meeting. Designers specify topics, project phase, time needed, and type of feedback sought. PM partners are invited to hear feedback directly.

**Product Briefs** follow a structured lifecycle: Problem, Press Release, Approach, Details, Journey, Retrospective. Teams use parts or the whole thing depending on effort size. Press releases are used early to explore broad directions and align on customer value.

**Launch Calendar:** A centralized source of truth for anything going out to customers -- features, improvements, partner launches, email campaigns, blog posts. Automated reminders keep details updated. Weekly summary emails of past and upcoming launches.

**Tool Stack:** Coda for basically everything -- team hubs organically grow from specs to task tables to embedded Figma prototypes to dashboards to retrospectives. This creates a historical record for institutional knowledge and eliminates the "which tool has this?" problem.

## What Makes Them Distinctive

1. **Dory and Pulse (Golden Ritual):** Every new employee knows this by their first Friday. Pulse: everyone writes down what they think with responses hidden until done, forcing eloquent, unbiased, on-the-record opinions. Dory (named after the fish who asks all questions): questions are submitted and up/down voted to determine discussion priorities. This eliminates groupthink and ensures new voices can outvote the CEO's question.

2. **"Right vs. Familiar" culture:** A core value where the team actively questions whether familiar approaches make sense or should be torn down and rebuilt from first principles. Applied to everything from company equity structure to product building blocks to tiny interaction details.

3. **Two-Way Writeups:** Coda's alternative to the traditional one-way presentation or memo. Documents are designed for interactive engagement -- embedded reactions (heart, question, clap), inline Dory and Pulse, and asynchronous feedback loops. One team famously rewrote "Hooked on a Feeling" lyrics for their OKR presentation.

4. **Eigenquestions framework:** From Shishir's YouTube experience -- finding the single question that, when answered, answers the most subsequent questions. At YouTube, the eigenquestion "Do we value consistency over comprehensiveness?" resolved dozens of contentious debates including whether to link out to ABC.com for Modern Family and whether to take back the YouTube iPhone app from Apple.

5. **Black Loop and Blue Loop growth model:** Black Loop (the Microsoft Loop) is the viral document sharing cycle: create, share, create. Blue Loop (the YouTube Loop) is public publishing where users publish docs to the world, exposing Coda to new audiences through problem-solving content rather than product marketing.

## What You Can Steal

1. **Implement Dory and Pulse in your meetings.** Before any discussion, have everyone write their position independently (hidden from others) then reveal simultaneously. Collect questions and vote on which to discuss. This eliminates anchoring bias and ensures quiet voices are heard.

2. **Use the 10% planning rule.** Cap your planning time at 10% of the execution period. If you are planning for a quarter, you get roughly 6-7 working days. This forces discipline and prevents planning from consuming the time it is supposed to optimize.

3. **Distinguish input vs. output metrics in OKRs.** Stop goaling teams on lagging output metrics they cannot directly move in a quarter (like activation). Instead, goal on input metrics within their control and separately validate the hypothesis that those inputs drive the desired outputs.

4. **Create a single team hub for each project.** Instead of scattering specs, tasks, dashboards, meeting notes, and retros across multiple tools, consolidate into one living document that grows organically with the project. New team members can reconstruct full decision history from a single place.

5. **Practice eigenquestions in low-stakes settings.** Train your team to find the one question that resolves the most downstream decisions. Practice with fun hypotheticals (Shishir uses "a teleportation device has been invented -- you get two questions, what do you ask?") before applying to real strategic debates.

## Recommended Books

| Book | Author | Why It's Relevant |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| High Output Management | Andy Grove | The intellectual foundation for Coda's output-oriented culture, where planning efficiency and managerial leverage are treated as first principles. |
| Radical Focus | Christina Wodtke | Directly relevant to Coda's rigorous OKR practice, including their rejection of the "70% is success" model in favour of 100% commitment. |
| Measure What Matters | John Doerr | Provides the OKR framework that Coda adopted and then evolved, offering context for understanding their distinction between input and output metrics. |
| The Build Trap | Melissa Perri | Explains how to escape the cycle of shipping features without impact, reinforcing Coda's philosophy that OKRs are not a strategy and planning must serve outcomes. |
