# How Canva Builds Product

> Company Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## Company Overview

Canva is a visual communications platform valued at over $42B, generating $3.3B in annual revenue, with 240M+ monthly active users and 2,500+ engineers. The company has been profitable for eight consecutive years and operates across 100+ languages and 50+ countries for print delivery. Founded in Australia by Melanie Perkins, Canva started as a yearbook publishing tool (Fusion Books) before pivoting to the broad design platform it is today. Their product organization is notable for a deeply mission-driven planning structure, a "Column B" philosophy of working backwards from an audacious long-term vision, a community-driven feature pipeline processing over a million requests per year, and a culture of celebrating ambitious goals with memorable rituals.

## Org Structure

- 2,500+ engineers across the company at current scale.
- The CEO (Melanie Perkins) remains deeply involved in product vision and strategy, setting the tone through vision decks and mission pillars.
- Product teams are organized around components of the company's mission pillars (e.g., "design anything," "every language," "every device," "publish anywhere").
- As the company scaled, they evolved from daily stand-ups to weekly, then monthly, then "season openers" -- company-wide gatherings where goals are reviewed, achievements celebrated, and new cycle goals are set.
- Leadership philosophy emphasizes authenticity over imported playbooks. They have repeatedly tried processes from other companies and found that only internally developed approaches that are "authentic to how we do things" tend to stick.
- Melanie describes the scaling challenge as "every time the company doubles in size, pretty much all your systems break." They continuously reinvent processes for each new layer of scale rather than adopting off-the-shelf frameworks.

## Planning Process

- **Mission-driven hierarchy:** A top-level mission ("empower the world to design") is broken into **mission pillars** (e.g., "design anything," "with every ingredient," "in every language," "on every device"). Each pillar has **successive annual goals** that move the company closer to fulfilling it.
- **"Column B" thinking:** Rather than looking at current capabilities and asking what can be done (Column A / "stacking bricks"), Canva starts by imagining the ideal future and works backwards (Column B / "building a castle on the hill"). Melanie has a literal 2050 vision wall in her home office.
- **"Chaos to Clarity" framework:** Every idea starts in chaos (an idea, problem, or belief) and is progressively clarified through incremental steps -- writing it down, creating a pitch deck, refining into designs, then a prototype. Each step "adds clarity." Vision decks are created for every major project kickoff.
- **"Wild success / terrible failure" exercises:** Teams regularly ask, "What is wild success for X in 10 years? What is terrible failure?" This forces long-term thinking before tactical planning.
- Goals are set as **Crazy Big Goals** (an actual company value). The team excels at achieving these goals but acknowledges timing estimates are often unreliable -- what they think will take six months may take a year or two.
- **Two sources of product ideas:** (1) Top-down vision and mission pillars driving the future roadmap, and (2) bottom-up community feedback -- over 1 million feature requests per year from users, tallied, broken down, and delivered to product teams. In a recent year they "closed the loop" on 200+ community-requested features.
- User testing is deeply embedded: Melanie has personally run "hundreds if not thousands" of user tests, primarily online via UserTesting.com.

## How They Execute

- **Season openers** serve as planning and celebration cadence. Teams present achieved goals and set goals for the coming cycle. These became unwieldy as the company grew (reaching six hours), requiring continuous format evolution.
- **Goal-driven shipping deadlines:** Teams naturally ship ahead of season openers because they want to present completed work, creating an organic forcing function.
- Features and products are frequently built during **focused one-week (or shorter) hackathon sprints**. These concentrated bursts of building have produced some of the most exciting and memorable work at the company.
- **Celebration rituals** are coupled with Crazy Big Goals. When a goal is achieved (e.g., launching in 100 languages, launching in Spanish), the team celebrates with memorable events -- smashing plates, releasing doves, La Tomatina festivals. This reinforces focus and gives teams moments of pride.
- AI is integrated across the entire product suite: users can generate presentations, videos, emails, websites, and code directly in the editor. AI is embedded in the design tab (170M uses/month) and elements tab (900M uses/month).
- During a two-year front-end rewrite (expected to take six months), the team gamified progress: they created a board game with rubber ducky bath toys representing components, with weekly stand-ups where everyone moved their toy along the board.
- Community feedback loop: a dedicated team processes 1M+ annual requests, routes them to product teams, and closes the loop when features ship.

## What Makes Them Distinctive

1. **"Column B" planning philosophy.** Instead of planning from current capabilities, Canva starts from an imagined ideal future and works backwards. Every major initiative begins with a vision deck painting the dream state before any tactical work begins.
2. **"Chaos to Clarity" as a universal process.** Every idea moves through progressive steps of clarification -- from a written note to a pitch deck to designs to a prototype. The framework gives people permission to start with "embarrassingly" vague ideas and incrementally make them concrete.
3. **Crazy Big Goals as a company value, paired with celebration rituals.** Goals are deliberately set to be intimidating ("you feel completely inadequate before it"), and every achievement is marked with a memorable celebration. This creates both motivation and recognition.
4. **Community-driven product pipeline at scale.** Over 1M feature requests per year are systematically processed, routed to product teams, and closed -- creating a direct feedback loop between users and shipping decisions. This is not suggestion-box theater; 200+ loops were closed in a single year.
5. **Mission pillars as a product expansion framework.** "Empower everyone to design anything with every ingredient in every language on every device" is taken literally as a product roadmap. Each word in the mission statement generates years of product work (e.g., "every language" drove expansion from English to 100+ languages including right-to-left scripts).

## What You Can Steal

1. **Start every major initiative with a vision deck.** Before any tactical planning, create a short deck painting the dream outcome. Ask "What is wild success in 10 years?" and "What is terrible failure?" to anchor the team on ambition before constraints.
2. **Use your mission statement as a literal product roadmap.** Break your mission into pillars, then take each word seriously as a multi-year product direction. Track annual progress against each pillar.
3. **Build a systematic community feedback loop.** Collect feature requests at scale, route them to product teams, and publicly close the loop when you ship what was requested. Even at Canva's scale (1M+ requests/year), they close 200+ loops annually.
4. **Couple ambitious goals with memorable celebrations.** When a Crazy Big Goal is achieved, mark it with something people will remember -- not just a Slack message, but a physical, shared experience. This creates motivation to ship and recognition when you do.
5. **Gamify long, painful infrastructure projects.** When you are in a multi-month grind with no visible shipping, find creative ways to make progress tangible and fun (like Canva's rubber ducky game board during their two-year rewrite).
