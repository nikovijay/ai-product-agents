# Playbook: Running Your First 0-to-1 Product

> Situation Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## The Situation

You have been handed the greenfield brief: build something new from scratch. Maybe it is a new product line within an established company, or you are the first PM at an early-stage startup. Either way, there is no existing product to optimize, no dashboard to check, no users to talk to. There is just a hypothesis, some ambition, and a blank canvas.

This is simultaneously the most thrilling and most terrifying assignment in product management. The thrill comes from the creative freedom -- you get to define the problem, design the solution, and build something that did not exist before. The terror comes from the absence of guardrails. There is no data to validate your decisions, no established process to follow, and no existing customers to tell you what they want. Every decision is high-stakes because there is no momentum to carry you through mistakes.

What makes 0-to-1 uniquely hard is that the skills that made you successful as a PM on a scaling product -- optimizing funnels, running A/B tests, managing a roadmap -- are largely irrelevant here. The 0-to-1 PM is more like a founder than a traditional product manager. You need strong opinions about what should exist in the world, the resilience to be wrong repeatedly, and the judgment to know when to push through resistance versus when to pivot. Most PMs who fail at 0-to-1 fail not because they lack skill, but because they apply the wrong playbook.

## The Playbook

### Phase 1: Discovery and Conviction (Month 1)

1. **Start with the problem, not the solution.** Use Lenny's problem crystallization framework: What is the problem? How do we know it is real? Who has it? Why is it worth solving now? Write this down in a single page. If you cannot articulate the problem in one sentence, you do not understand it well enough.
2. **Talk to 30+ potential users in 30 days.** This is not optional. Do not build anything until you have deep qualitative evidence that the problem is real, painful, and frequent. As the founders of Gong, Gusto, and Retool showed, obsessive early customer contact is the single biggest predictor of finding product-market fit.
3. **Identify your initial customer persona.** You cannot build for everyone. Pick the narrowest, most specific audience that has the most acute version of the problem. As Lenny's B2B series shows, the journey to PMF starts by getting one company (or one user segment) to love your product.
4. **Develop a strong point of view.** Based on your research, articulate what you believe to be true that most people do not. This is your strategic insight. Without it, you are just building features.
5. **Write a vision document.** Jackie Bavaro recommends sketching a storyboard of your future customer using your future product. Spend time making the current pain vivid before describing the future state.
6. **Assemble your team.** Fight for the smallest possible team of the strongest possible people. As Lenny's research shows, a small hungry entrepreneurial team with a clear mandate is the optimal 0-to-1 structure.

### Phase 2: Build and Validate (Month 1-3)

1. **Ship an MVP in weeks, not months.** Lenny's B2B research found that most successful startups got an alpha product out in 1-3 months. Your MVP should be embarrassingly simple -- it needs to test your core hypothesis, nothing more.
2. **Do things that do not scale.** Before building automation, do things manually. Figma's founders obsessively supported their first customer, Coda. Retool built custom analytics to watch every user session in real time. Gusto's founders sat in their customers' offices watching them run payroll.
3. **Define your success criteria before launch.** What specific, measurable outcome would tell you this is working? Write it down. As Lenny advises: "Did I do that or did I not do that? Yes? No? Simple."
4. **Create tight feedback loops.** Be in constant contact with your first users. Watch them use the product. Call them when they stop. The Retool team shipped fixes within 36 hours of a customer discovering a problem. That cadence of responsiveness is what 0-to-1 demands.
5. **Use the 80/20 approach for big bets.** Lenny's "defending your big bets" strategy applies here: spend 80% of effort on the core MVP and 20% on quick wins that demonstrate progress to stakeholders and maintain organizational support.
6. **Create cover fire.** If you are within a larger company, consistently ship small visible wins alongside your big bet. This buys you the time and resources to pursue the 0-to-1 vision.

### Phase 3: Find Fit or Pivot (Month 3-6)

1. **Evaluate product-market fit honestly.** Look for the shift from push to pull. Are users coming back without being asked? Are they telling others? Are they angry when the product breaks? If not, you have not found fit yet.
2. **Get one user to truly love your product.** This is the first step in Lenny's PMF framework. Do whatever it takes to make one customer wildly successful. Their passion becomes your proof of concept.
3. **Know the difference between a bad idea and bad execution.** When early results are disappointing, resist the urge to pivot immediately. As Lenny writes: "Sometimes it is because the idea was bad, but sometimes it is because it was executed badly." Examine which assumptions were validated and which were not.
4. **Be willing to pivot, but do it with data.** If after meaningful effort you cannot find a single user who loves the product, it is time to change direction. But pivot on the solution, not the problem. The problem you identified is likely still real -- you just may not have found the right solution.
5. **Set a timeline for PMF.** Lenny's research found that the median time from idea to feeling PMF in B2B was about two years, and from working product to PMF was 9-18 months. Start worrying if you have been at it for over two years without feeling pull.
6. **Graduate from 0-to-1 when you have repeatable evidence of value.** PMF is not a binary moment -- it is a process of finding fit with larger segments. Move to scaling mode when you have multiple customers who love the product and you understand why they love it.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Archive

1. **"The journey to PMF starts by getting one company to truly love your product."** Do whatever it takes to make them successful. Figma's Dylan Field obsessed over their first user, treating any bug as a red alert. (Source: `a-guide-for-finding-product-market-fit-in-b2b.md`)

2. **"PMF is never a binary yes-or-no moment."** It is a gradual process of finding fit with larger segments. Even at 100 million DAU, YouTube never felt they had PMF with their desired audience. (Source: `a-guide-for-finding-product-market-fit-in-b2b.md`)

3. **"Keep it small, avoid too much attention until ready."** Sriram Krishnan's advice for big bets within large companies. A small hungry team with a clear mandate and independent operations is optimal for 0-to-1. (Source: `defending-your-big-bets.md`)

4. **"Nothing is more certain to cause a project to fail than a misunderstanding of the problem you are solving."** Lenny learned this at Airbnb when his team built a social travel feature solving the wrong problem. The real problem was not "travelers want to meet other travelers" but "travelers want non-touristy things to do." (Source: `a-three-step-framework-for-solving-problems.md`)

5. **"A strategy is your plan to win."** For 0-to-1, your strategy should include mission, vision, and a clear set of strategic bets. The Airbnb Instant Book team used a simple but powerful strategy framework: identify the problem, envision the ideal state, and carve the path between the two. (Source: `getting-better-at-product-strategy.md`)

6. **Retool's David Hsu built real-time customer monitoring into the product from day one:** "Anytime anyone was in the app, we got notified on Slack. Invariably, they'd run into issues, and we'd immediately reach out. We'd just call them." This level of responsiveness is what separates successful 0-to-1 products. (Source: `a-guide-for-finding-product-market-fit-in-b2b.md`)

7. **"There are seven common ways to differentiate."** Michael Porter's insight via Lenny: be cheapest, highest quality, most convenient, safest, proprietary, feel-good, or niche. Pick one and go all-in. (Source: `differentiating-your-product.md`)

8. **Gusto's founders sat in customer offices watching payroll being run:** "For the first 30 customers, I was with them seeing them do payroll, or I was on the phone with them, watching them and seeing their reactions." (Source: `a-guide-for-finding-product-market-fit-in-b2b.md`)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Building too much before talking to users.** The most common 0-to-1 failure mode is spending months building a product nobody wants. Lenny's research shows successful founders got MVPs out in 1-3 months and iterated from there.

2. **Solving for everyone instead of someone.** Trying to build for a broad audience dilutes your value proposition. Start with the narrowest possible segment that has the most acute pain.

3. **Confusing a solution with a problem.** Lenny's Airbnb social travel story is the canonical example. "Build a loyalty program" is a solution masquerading as a problem statement.

4. **Giving up too early on a good idea.** Some of the most successful products (Figma, Airtable, Slack) took 4+ years to find PMF. Be honest about whether you are iterating on the right problem, but do not panic at the first sign of difficulty.

5. **Not creating organizational cover.** Within a large company, 0-to-1 projects are easy targets when budgets tighten. Build trust by shipping small wins alongside the big bet, and keep leadership informed of progress and learnings.

## Signs You're on Track

1. Users are voluntarily coming back to the product without being prompted or incentivized.
2. At least one user or customer has said some version of "I would be upset if this product went away."
3. You are learning something new and meaningful from each user interaction, and that learning is changing what you build.
4. The team is energized and moving fast, shipping updates in days rather than weeks.
5. You can clearly articulate the problem you are solving, who you are solving it for, and why your approach is differentiated -- and the evidence supports each claim.

## Signs You're Off Track

1. You have been building for more than three months without putting the product in front of real users.
2. Users try the product once and do not come back, and you do not know why.
3. You are spending more time on internal presentations and stakeholder management than on customer discovery and product iteration.
4. The team is debating features rather than testing hypotheses.
5. You cannot name a single user who genuinely loves the product.
