# Playbook: Transitioning from IC to Manager

> Situation Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## The Situation

You have been a strong individual contributor PM -- maybe one of the best on your team. You have shipped products, hit goals, and earned the trust of your engineers and designers. Now someone has asked you to manage other PMs. Congratulations. You are about to discover that almost nothing you learned as an IC directly prepares you for this role.

The transition from IC PM to PM manager is one of the most disorienting career shifts in product management. As Lenny writes, "In one sense managing PMs is the same job as an IC PM -- marshaling the resources of your team to ship product and deliver business impact. In practice, it is completely different." Instead of working directly on the product, everything now happens indirectly, through your PM team. The habits that made you successful as an IC -- deep customer knowledge, hands-on roadmap ownership, being the person with all the answers -- can actually become liabilities if you do not let go of them.

What makes this transition emotionally difficult is the loss of direct craft. As an IC, you got daily satisfaction from writing a sharp strategy doc, shipping a feature, or uncovering a customer insight. As a manager, your wins are slower, less visible, and mediated through others. Many new managers describe feeling useless for the first few months -- attending meetings but not "doing" anything. This is normal. The most common mistake is to cope with this discomfort by continuing to do IC work, which simultaneously starves your reports of autonomy and prevents you from developing the new skills you actually need.

## The Playbook

### Phase 1: Shift Your Mindset (Month 1)

1. **Accept that this is a fundamentally different role.** Lenny's essay on IC to manager makes this point emphatically: the transition is not a promotion into a bigger version of the same job. It is a career change. The skills are different, the daily work is different, and the definition of success is different.
2. **Internalize the leverage equation.** Borrowing from Andy Grove: "A PM manager's output = the output of their team + the output of the neighboring teams under their influence." Your job is no longer to produce great work directly, but to multiply the quality and impact of the work produced by others.
3. **Stop doing IC work.** This is the hardest step. Do not write PRDs for your reports. Do not take over their design reviews. Do not attend their standups. If you find yourself doing IC work, ask: am I doing this because it needs to be done, or because it makes me feel productive?
4. **Set up 1:1s with each of your direct reports.** Understand their goals, strengths, challenges, and working style. Ask them what kind of support they need from you. Listen more than you talk.
5. **Build your new leadership team.** Your team is now your peer managers -- the EM, design manager, data science manager. As Lenny writes, "it is essential that you work as one unit because your team will be looking to each of you for a clear direction."
6. **Read the essential books.** Lenny recommends: The Making of a Manager by Julie Zhou, Radical Candor by Kim Scott, High Output Management by Andy Grove, and Quiet Leadership by David Rock.

### Phase 2: Learn to Lead Through Others (Month 1-3)

1. **Teach, do not do.** When a report brings you a problem, your instinct will be to solve it. Instead, ask questions that help them solve it themselves. "What options have you considered? What is your recommendation? What would you need to feel confident in this decision?" As Lenny writes, "It is more about teaching than doing."
2. **Develop your coaching muscle.** Each 1:1 should include some element of coaching. Share frameworks, give feedback on documents and presentations, and help your reports think through ambiguous situations. The goal is to make each PM better, not to make their decisions for them.
3. **Establish a high quality bar.** Lenny identifies two quality bars you must preserve: PM team quality and product quality. Review strategy documents, roadmaps, and presentations from your reports. Push back when the work is not sharp enough. Ask: "What is one thing we could do to push this one step further?"
4. **Unblock, unblock, unblock.** Lenny calls this one of the top five unexpected jobs of a PM manager. Use your increased influence and authority to unblock your teams at every level -- short-term decisions, medium-term hiring, long-term strategy alignment.
5. **Start having skip-level conversations.** Saumil Mehta's framework from his Square experience: have recurring 1:1s with your skip reports, but be careful not to undermine your direct report managers. Listen carefully, ask follow-up questions, but work through your managers to act on what you learn.
6. **Manage up effectively.** You are now the bridge between your team and senior leadership. Keep your manager informed of what is going well, what is at risk, and where you need support. Use the autonomy template from Jackie Bavaro: "Here is a challenge, here is how I am handling it, any thoughts?"

### Phase 3: Build for Scale (Month 3-6)

1. **Develop a team strategy.** Your individual PMs own their product strategies. You own the team strategy -- how the pieces fit together, where the gaps are, and what the team should collectively accomplish over the next year.
2. **Invest in hiring.** As Lenny notes, preserving and improving the PM team quality bar is a core responsibility. Define what great looks like at each level, build a hiring process, and recruit actively. Your team is only as strong as its weakest PM.
3. **Create scalable processes.** Establish how your team does planning, goal-setting, design reviews, stakeholder communication, and post-mortems. These processes should create clarity without becoming bureaucratic.
4. **Give hard feedback.** Alisa Cohn's scripts for difficult conversations are essential here. Your job is not to make people happy -- it is to help them get better. Be direct, be kind, and be specific. As Lenny learned at Airbnb, "Leaders respect people who are clear-headed, upfront, and take responsibility."
5. **Never undermine, never cover.** Saumil Mehta's two cardinal rules for managing managers: do not undermine your direct reports by making decisions around them, and do not cover for their underperformance. Both create dysfunction that compounds over time.
6. **Use task-relevant maturity to delegate.** Andy Grove's concept (via Saumil Mehta): match the difficulty of each project to the skill level of the manager taking it on. High-uncertainty, high-impact projects go to your most experienced people. Lower-stakes work can develop newer managers.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Archive

1. **"A PM manager's output = the output of their team + the output of the neighboring teams under their influence."** This Andy Grove paraphrase from Lenny's IC-to-manager piece captures the entire mindset shift. Your output is no longer your own work. (Source: `moving-from-ic-product-manager-to-manager-of-product-managers.md`)

2. **"Your job is to stop stupid sh*t from happening."** As a manager, you are in a higher echelon of influence. Use it. When you see something going wrong, push back and ask direct questions. (Source: `moving-from-ic-product-manager-to-manager-of-product-managers.md`)

3. **"All manager challenges are either directly or indirectly about the skip lead."** Saumil Mehta's insight that the quality of a manager is ultimately the responsibility of their manager. This reframes your own accountability. (Source: `five-principles-for-successfully-managing-managers.md`)

4. **"The notion that the learning curve from line manager to manager of managers is just as steep -- if not steeper -- is highly counterintuitive."** Even if this is your second management transition, take it seriously. Each level requires a different skill set. (Source: `five-principles-for-successfully-managing-managers.md`)

5. **The "API endpoint experiment":** If you could only interact with your direct reports (not their ICs), how would you be effective? This thought experiment from Saumil Mehta forces you to develop true management skills rather than continuing to do IC work through your reports. (Source: `five-principles-for-successfully-managing-managers.md`)

6. **Jackie Bavaro's mistake of saying no to everything:** In her first year at Asana, Jackie defaulted to shutting down ideas to protect the roadmap. Her coach challenged her to "say yes to everything for two weeks" and she discovered that the protectiveness was preventing real collaboration. New managers often repeat this pattern with their reports. (Source: `jackie-bavaro.md`)

7. **Ami Vora's curiosity principle:** "Fascinating, tell me more why you think that." The best leaders respond to disagreement with genuine curiosity rather than defensiveness. This approach tears down walls between points of view. (Source: `ami-vora.md`)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Continuing to do IC work.** This is the single most common failure mode for new PM managers. It prevents your reports from growing, prevents you from developing management skills, and exhausts you.

2. **Trying to make everyone happy.** Lenny warns that "the default path is to make everyone feel good by letting OK work slide. The more courageous path is to push everyone around you to keep the bar as high as possible."

3. **Micromanaging.** The opposite failure mode of doing IC work. Do not tell your reports exactly what to build or how to run their teams. Set direction, establish the quality bar, and let them figure out the how.

4. **Not investing in your peer relationships.** Your leadership team (EM, design manager, etc.) is now your primary team. If this group is not aligned and cohesive, your entire organization will be dysfunctional.

5. **Avoiding difficult conversations.** New managers often delay giving hard feedback because they want to be liked. But as Alisa Cohn teaches, not giving someone the opportunity to hear what they need to hear prevents them from growing -- and that is the real unkindness.

## Signs You're on Track

1. Your direct reports are shipping strong work and making good decisions without your direct involvement in the details.
2. You are spending the majority of your time on coaching, strategy, hiring, and cross-functional alignment -- not on IC work.
3. Your peer managers (EM, design) describe the leadership team as aligned and effective.
4. Your reports tell you that 1:1s are valuable and that they feel supported and challenged.
5. You have a clear team strategy that your manager has endorsed and your reports are executing against.

## Signs You're Off Track

1. You are writing PRDs, attending standups, or making product decisions that should be made by your reports.
2. Your reports are not growing -- they are doing the same quality of work they were doing six months ago.
3. You are overwhelmed by meetings and feel like you are not adding value in any of them.
4. Your leadership team (you, EM, design manager) is not meeting regularly or is frequently misaligned.
5. You avoid giving feedback because you do not want to create conflict, and performance issues are going unaddressed.

## Recommended Books

| Book | Author | Why It's Relevant |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| The Making of a Manager | Julie Zhuo | Written by a designer who became a VP at Facebook, this is the most practical guide to the IC-to-manager transition -- covering everything from 1:1s to giving feedback to knowing when you are doing well. |
| Radical Candor | Kim Scott | Teaches the "care personally, challenge directly" framework that new managers need to give honest feedback without being either a pushover or a tyrant. |
| High Output Management | Andy Grove | The origin of the leverage equation that defines management: your output equals the output of your team. Essential mental model for anyone making this shift. |
| The Five Dysfunctions of a Team | Patrick Lencioni | As a new manager, building a cohesive leadership triad is your top priority -- this book diagnoses why teams fail and how to fix trust, conflict, and accountability. |
| An Elegant Puzzle | Will Larson | A systems-thinking approach to engineering management that covers team sizing, organizational design, and the process challenges you will face as you scale a PM team. |
| Empowered | Marty Cagan | Focuses specifically on what it means to lead product teams rather than manage them, including coaching PMs, setting context, and preserving team autonomy. |
