# Playbook: Turning Around a Struggling Product

> Situation Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## The Situation

The metrics are declining. Leadership is nervous. The team is demoralized. Maybe you were brought in specifically to fix this, or maybe you were already on the team when things started to slip. Either way, you are now the person responsible for a product that is losing users, missing goals, or failing to deliver on its promise. Everyone is watching, and opinions about what went wrong are everywhere.

Turnarounds are among the hardest assignments in product management because they combine strategic complexity with emotional difficulty. You need to diagnose the root cause while simultaneously managing anxious stakeholders, rebuilding a demoralized team, and delivering quick wins that demonstrate momentum. The temptation is to swing between two extremes: either declaring everything broken and proposing a complete overhaul, or making incremental tweaks that avoid confronting the real issues. Neither works. The best turnaround PMs find the third path -- an honest diagnosis paired with a phased plan that delivers early credibility while solving deeper structural problems.

What makes this especially challenging is that a struggling product often has struggling relationships behind it. When things go wrong, trust erodes -- between PM and engineering, between the team and leadership, between the company and its users. Your job is not just to fix the product but to rebuild these relationships. That requires a particular kind of courage: the willingness to acknowledge what is not working, to have difficult conversations, and to take responsibility for the path forward even when the problems were not yours to begin with.

## The Playbook

### Phase 1: Diagnose Honestly (Week 1-3)

1. **Conduct a ruthless assessment of the current state.** Pull the data. What are the actual trends for key metrics -- retention, engagement, revenue, NPS? Separate the narratives from the numbers. Often, the team's story about what is happening does not match what the data shows.
2. **Talk to churned and unhappy users.** The most valuable signal in a turnaround comes from people who left or are about to leave. Set up 10-15 conversations with churned users and ask: What made you leave? What would bring you back? What were you hoping for that you did not get?
3. **Interview the team.** Talk to every engineer, designer, and stakeholder privately. Ask: What do you think is the core problem? What have we tried that did not work, and why? What would you do if you had full authority? You will often find that the team already knows the answer -- they just were not empowered to act on it.
4. **Map the competitive landscape.** Has the market shifted? Have competitors launched something better? Is the category itself declining? Sometimes a product is not struggling because of internal failures but because the external environment changed.
5. **Separate the problem from the solution.** As Lenny learned at Airbnb, "nothing is more certain to cause a project to fail than a misunderstanding of the problem you are solving." Before proposing fixes, make sure you understand whether the product is solving the wrong problem, solving the right problem badly, or solving the right problem for the wrong audience.
6. **Write a one-page diagnosis.** Articulate the root cause(s) clearly and concisely. This document becomes the foundation for everything that follows.

### Phase 2: Stabilize and Win Quick (Week 3-8)

1. **Communicate the diagnosis transparently.** Lenny's advice on communicating failure is critical here: be upfront, stay positive, separate the idea from the execution, and have a clear plan for next steps. Leaders respect people who are clear-headed and take responsibility.
2. **Identify and ship 2-3 quick wins.** These should be high-impact, low-effort improvements that demonstrate momentum. Fix the most complained-about bug. Improve the most-used but worst-performing flow. Ship something users have been asking for that was deprioritized.
3. **Stop the bleeding on the worst metric.** If retention is the problem, focus all energy there. If it is activation, fix onboarding. Do not try to fix everything at once. Ruthless prioritization is your greatest weapon.
4. **Rebuild team morale through early wins.** Nothing restores a demoralized team like shipping something that works. Celebrate small wins publicly. As Lenny writes, great PMs "amplify the successes and contributions of team members."
5. **Manage stakeholder expectations.** Use Jules Walter's influence framework: seek intel on what leadership cares about most, frame your plan from their perspective, and prime them before formal reviews. Share the diagnosis and plan proactively -- do not wait for them to come asking.
6. **Kill features or projects that are not working.** A turnaround often requires subtraction, not addition. Identify the features, initiatives, or targets that are consuming resources without delivering value, and have the courage to cut them.

### Phase 3: Rebuild Strategically (Month 2-4)

1. **Draft a new strategy based on your diagnosis.** Use Lenny's strategy framework: Mission (what are we trying to achieve?) to Vision (what does success look like?) to Strategy (how will we get there?) to Goals (how will we measure progress?) to Roadmap (what do we build?).
2. **Pick one or two high-leverage strategic bets.** As Richard Rumelt teaches (via Lenny's strategy guide), good strategy "identifies the one or two critical issues -- the pivot points that can multiply the effectiveness of effort -- and then focuses and concentrates action and resources on them."
3. **Align the team around a new narrative.** The old narrative was about decline. You need a new narrative about where the product is going and why it will win. Lenny's 14 habits piece emphasizes: "great PMs frequently remind teammates of how their work connects to the mission."
4. **Re-establish the product quality bar.** As Lenny writes about the PM manager role, your job is to "preserve and improve the product quality bar." Push the team to think deeper and go further. When reviewing work, ask: "What is one thing we can do to push this just one step further?"
5. **Set new goals and a 90-day checkpoint.** Define clear, measurable goals for the turnaround. Schedule a review at 90 days to assess progress honestly and adjust the strategy.
6. **Invest in the team.** A struggling product often has talent gaps. Identify where the team needs strengthening -- whether through hiring, skill development, or restructuring responsibilities.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Archive

1. **"Failure is super normal. A large percentage of technology projects fail."** Lenny's advice on communicating bad news: do not equate a project's failure with your personal failure. Be upfront, stay positive, and have a clear plan for next steps. (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

2. **"Be up front. The truth will come out."** When framing bad news to leadership: describe the journey, share what worked, and present a clear recommendation for next steps. "This is how you turn a failure into an opportunity." (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

3. **"Spend 80% on short-term low-risk incremental wins, and 20% on high-risk, long-term bets."** Lenny's formula for maintaining organizational support. In a turnaround, the quick wins are even more important because they buy you the credibility to pursue the bigger strategic changes. (Source: `defending-your-big-bets.md`)

4. **"A strategy is your plan to win."** Turnarounds need a new strategy, not just new tactics. Good strategy is problem-oriented, insight-driven, actionable, focused, and cohesive. (Source: `getting-better-at-product-strategy.md`)

5. **"Great PMs ruthlessly prioritize, both the team's work and their own."** There are always more ideas than time. The greatest gift you can give a struggling team is focus. (Source: `14-habits-of-highly-effective-product-managers.md`)

6. **"Great PMs hunt for misalignment and quickly push everyone back into alignment."** Team dysfunction is often rooted in people having different information, motives, or priorities. Rooting these out is high-leverage turnaround work. (Source: `14-habits-of-highly-effective-product-managers.md`)

7. **Managing founder expectations during a pivot:** "Bring them on the journey with you. Align on key hypotheses and test them one by one. Stay optimistic. Show irrefutable data." (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Proposing a complete rewrite or overhaul.** Camille Fournier warns on Lenny's podcast that major rewrites are often a trap. They take longer than expected, deliver less than promised, and demoralize the team. Fix what you have before rebuilding from scratch.

2. **Blaming the previous PM or team.** Even if they made mistakes, blame destroys trust. Focus on the path forward, not assigning fault for the past.

3. **Trying to fix everything simultaneously.** A turnaround demands brutal prioritization. Pick the one or two highest-leverage problems and focus all energy there.

4. **Ignoring the emotional state of the team.** A demoralized team cannot execute a turnaround. Invest in morale, celebrate small wins, and be transparent about the challenges ahead.

5. **Skipping the diagnosis phase.** The pressure to "do something" is intense. But acting without understanding the root cause often makes things worse. Take the time to diagnose before prescribing.

## Signs You're on Track

1. The key declining metric has stabilized or begun to improve.
2. The team is shipping regularly again and morale is visibly improving.
3. Churned users are returning, or churn rate is declining.
4. Stakeholders are expressing confidence in the direction, even if results are not yet dramatic.
5. You have a clear strategy document that the team is aligned around and excited about.

## Signs You're Off Track

1. After six weeks, you still cannot articulate the root cause of the decline.
2. Quick wins are not moving the needle and the team is losing faith in the plan.
3. Stakeholders are escalating concerns or proposing their own solutions around you.
4. The team is still debating the diagnosis rather than executing the plan.
5. Top talent is leaving or disengaging.
