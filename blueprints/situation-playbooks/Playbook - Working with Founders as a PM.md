# Playbook: Working with Founders as a PM

> Situation Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## The Situation

You are a product manager at a company where the founder (or founders) are deeply involved in the product. Maybe you are the first PM hire at a startup, brought in to add structure to a founder-driven product process. Maybe you are a senior PM at a scaled company where the CEO still has strong opinions about every feature. Either way, you find yourself in one of the most nuanced and politically complex roles in product management: adding value to a product that someone else conceived, built, and feels enormous ownership over.

The fundamental tension of this role is that the PM discipline is built on the premise that the PM drives the product vision, strategy, and roadmap. But when you work with a founder-CEO, they often hold that role -- and they have something you do not: the authority of having created the company, the credibility of having bet their career on the idea, and (frequently) the final say on everything. Your challenge is to make yourself indispensable without stepping on the vision that predates you.

What makes this situation emotionally taxing is the ambiguity of your authority. You may be told you "own" the product, but the founder can override any decision. You may be asked for your recommendation, but the founder may have already decided. You may spend weeks on a strategy that the founder rewrites on a napkin in five minutes. The PMs who thrive in this environment are the ones who reframe the relationship: you are not there to own the vision -- you are there to make the founder's vision real, to bring discipline to their intuition, and to tell them what they need to hear even when it is uncomfortable. That last part is the hardest and the most valuable.

## The Playbook

### Phase 1: Understand the Founder (Month 1)

1. **Study how the founder makes decisions.** Jules Walter's first influence tactic is directly applicable here: seek intel on how each stakeholder makes decisions. What does the founder value most -- data, customer stories, competitive analysis, gut instinct? Who do they consult? What are they afraid of? Keep a notes doc where you write down the questions they ask and the feedback they give in meetings.
2. **Learn the origin story deeply.** Why did the founder start this company? What problem were they personally trying to solve? What early decisions shaped the product's DNA? The more you understand the founder's original insight, the better you can extend it rather than contradict it.
3. **Identify the founder's product superpowers and blind spots.** Every founder has both. Maybe they have extraordinary design taste but ignore operational complexity. Maybe they have deep technical insight but struggle to understand the average user. Your job is to complement their strengths, not compete with them.
4. **Build personal trust before professional trust.** Have conversations that are not about work. Understand what motivates them beyond the company. As Ami Vora describes, the thing that determines how good you are at a job is whether you feel trust with the people around you.
5. **Understand the founder's relationship with the board and investors.** Much of a founder's behavior is shaped by pressures you may not see. Board expectations, fundraising timelines, and investor feedback all influence their decisions. The more you understand this context, the better you can frame your own recommendations.
6. **Observe before asserting.** Spend your first month absorbing the founder's decision-making patterns, communication style, and priorities. As Lenny advises for any new role, listen before you lead.

### Phase 2: Earn Your Seat (Month 1-3)

1. **Make the founder's ideas better, not different.** Your instinct may be to bring your own vision. Resist this initially. Instead, take the founder's ideas and make them more specific, more testable, and more executable. Add the rigor that founders often skip: customer validation, success metrics, competitive analysis, and implementation considerations.
2. **Bring unique value the founder cannot replicate.** The founder already has vision and authority. What they typically lack is systematic customer research, data analysis, cross-functional coordination, and operational discipline. Become the person who brings irrefutable customer insight to every conversation.
3. **Frame your recommendations from their perspective.** Jules Walter's second influence tactic: "It is often more effective to speak their language and demonstrate how your proposal will help them reach their goals, not yours." When the Slack monetization team needed CEO Stewart Butterfield's approval, they framed the entire proposal around user value (his priority), not revenue (their goal).
4. **Use the "meeting before the meeting."** Jules Walter's third tactic is critical when working with founders. Before any high-stakes discussion, prime the founder with your thinking, hear their concerns, and adjust your approach. The goal is to walk into the room having already resolved the major objections.
5. **Own the recommendation, let the founder own the decision.** Ami Vora's principle -- "my manager owns context, I own the recommendation" -- is the perfect framing for this relationship. Do the deep work, synthesize the options, make a clear recommendation, and then genuinely respect the founder's right to decide differently.
6. **Build a track record of being right.** The fastest way to earn a founder's trust is to make predictions that come true. When you recommend something and it works, document it. Over time, this track record becomes the foundation of your credibility.

### Phase 3: Become a Trusted Partner (Month 3-6)

1. **Start telling the founder what they need to hear.** Once you have earned trust, the most valuable thing you can do is provide honest, clear-headed feedback that other people are afraid to give. Lenny's advice on managing founder expectations: "Bring them on the journey with you. Align on key hypotheses and test them one by one. Stay optimistic. Show irrefutable data."
2. **Help the founder change their mind without losing face.** Lenny's specific tactic: "Give the founder a chance to save face. It is difficult for anyone to admit they are wrong. Knowing this, find a way to make it feel like less of a failure and more of an evolution based on new learnings."
3. **Use data to anchor difficult conversations.** Founders are often driven by conviction and intuition. When you need to challenge their direction, lead with data -- both quantitative and qualitative. As Lenny advises, "work backward from what a compelling case for pivoting would be, and then go get that evidence."
4. **Find someone the founder trusts to reinforce your message.** Lenny's advice: "It is often tough for an IC on the team to convince the founder of anything they do not want to believe. Does the founder have a trusted advisor that you can pitch on your perspective, and then work together to influence the founder?"
5. **Accept that some battles are not worth fighting.** Lenny's final piece of advice on managing founders: "Accept they may never change their mind. If you begin to feel that is the case, step one is to recognize that, and step two is to decide if you want to continue working in that environment."
6. **Expand your influence gradually.** As the founder's trust in you grows, take on larger areas of product ownership. But always maintain the partnership dynamic -- you are extending their vision, not replacing it.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Archive

1. **"When looking to change someone's mind about something they firmly believe in: bring them on the journey with you."** It takes time to change a founder's mind. Share data points and learnings as you experience them, with your takeaways, and always with "here is what we are doing next to continue learning." (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

2. **"Align on the key hypotheses and test them one by one."** Something is keeping the founder convinced their idea is right. Listen to these insights, and the assumptions behind them. Together, come up with tests to vet these assumptions. Ask what would convince them that maybe it is not right. (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

3. **"Give the founder a chance to save face."** Make direction changes feel like evolutions based on new learning, not admissions of failure: "You made a decision based on prior information, and it was the right one given what you knew, but now that we know more, it makes sense to change direction." (Source: `this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)

4. **Jules Walter on influence at Slack:** When the CEO had reservations about a monetization experiment, the team framed the entire proposal around user benefits (the CEO's priority) rather than revenue impact (the team's goal). This led to a 20% increase in paying teams. (Source: `a-pms-guide-to-influence.md`)

5. **"Assume that executives have a little tiny dinosaur brain."** Ami Vora's principle that leadership has limited bandwidth. The best service you can provide is not information -- it is a clear recommendation. This is especially true when working with founders who have a thousand things competing for their attention. (Source: `ami-vora.md`)

6. **Carole Robin on co-founder dynamics:** "Most interpersonal issues have an interpersonal component. It is not just that you tend to dominate or that I tend to go quiet. Allowing for the possibility that we are both responsible reduces the tendency for accusation and increases the chance of joint problem-solving." This applies to PM-founder dynamics too. (Source: `healing-your-co-founder-relationship.md`)

7. **"People often won't listen to you until they feel that you've fully heard them."** Jules Walter's fourth influence tactic. When founders feel dismissed, they become more entrenched. The fastest way to influence a founder is to first make them feel deeply understood. (Source: `a-pms-guide-to-influence.md`)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Trying to "own" the product vision.** At a founder-led company, the founder owns the vision. Your job is to make it real, challenge it constructively, and extend it -- not replace it. PMs who come in trying to assert their own vision typically fail fast.

2. **Leading with what is wrong.** Founders built this thing from nothing. Starting with criticism -- even well-intentioned criticism -- triggers defensiveness. Lead with genuine curiosity about their thinking, then introduce data and alternative perspectives.

3. **Assuming the founder will be rational about data.** Founders have deep emotional attachment to their product. Data alone will not change their mind. You need data plus empathy plus the right framing plus time.

4. **Not building relationships with the people the founder trusts.** At a founder-led company, influence often flows through advisors, board members, and early employees. Build those relationships -- they can amplify your message in ways that a direct approach cannot.

5. **Giving up too easily when overruled.** Being overruled once does not mean you were wrong or that you should stop pushing. The best PM-founder relationships involve regular, healthy disagreement. Document your recommendations, respect the decision, and revisit when new evidence emerges.

## Signs You're on Track

1. The founder is proactively sharing early-stage thinking with you and asking for your input before decisions are made.
2. You have successfully influenced at least one meaningful product decision by bringing data or customer insight the founder did not have.
3. The founder references your work or analysis when speaking to the board, investors, or the broader team.
4. Engineers and designers come to you for direction and see you as a credible counterpart to the founder on product decisions.
5. The founder trusts you enough to disagree with you openly and constructively, rather than simply overruling silently.

## Signs You're Off Track

1. The founder consistently makes product decisions without consulting you or hearing your input.
2. You find yourself executing the founder's ideas without adding your own perspective or analysis.
3. Engineers and designers view you as a project manager -- someone who tracks tasks -- rather than a product leader.
4. You are afraid to share data or opinions that contradict the founder's direction.
5. The founder has a "shadow PM" (often a co-founder, early employee, or chief of staff) who they actually consult on product decisions.

## Recommended Reading from Lenny's Archive

1. **"Communicating bad news" (Issue 7)** -- Lenny's most directly relevant advice on managing founder expectations, changing their mind about a core idea, and what to do when the founder will not budge. (`this-week-7-effectively-communicating-about-a-failure-to-execs-managing-founder.md`)
2. **"A PM's guide to influence" by Jules Walter** -- The five influence tactics are arguably the most useful single piece for any PM working with founders. The Slack CEO case study is directly applicable. (`a-pms-guide-to-influence.md`)
3. **"Healing your co-founder relationship" by Carole Robin** -- While written for co-founders, the interpersonal frameworks (sticking to your reality, staying on your side of the net, joint problem-solving) apply directly to PM-founder dynamics. (`healing-your-co-founder-relationship.md`)
4. **Ami Vora podcast episode** -- The curiosity principle, dinosaur brain framework, and "my manager owns context, I own the recommendation" philosophy. A masterclass in working with powerful stakeholders. (`ami-vora.md`)
5. **"Becoming a senior Product Manager" by Jackie Bavaro** -- The autonomy template ("Here is a challenge, here is how I am handling it, any thoughts?") is perfectly designed for communicating with founders. (`becoming-a-senior-product-manager.md`)
6. **"14 habits of highly effective product managers"** -- Habit #5 (always have a point of view, loosely held) and habit #12 (make sure everyone has a chance to be heard) are essential for the PM-founder dynamic. (`14-habits-of-highly-effective-product-managers.md`)
7. **Nikhyl Singhal podcast episode** -- On career decisions, working at different company stages, and why diverse experiences matter. Helpful for deciding whether a founder-led company is the right environment for you. (`nikhyl-singhal.md`)
8. **Alisa Cohn podcast episode** -- Scripts for managing up, including her "founder prenup" concept and advice on having difficult conversations with company leaders. (`alisa-cohn.md`)
9. **"Five principles for successfully managing managers"** -- The "never undermine" principle applies in reverse when you are the one being managed by a founder. Understanding how organizational dynamics flow helps you navigate founder-led environments. (`five-principles-for-successfully-managing-managers.md`)
