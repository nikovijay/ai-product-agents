# Playbook: First 90 Days as a New PM

> Situation Playbook | Source: Lenny's Newsletter & Podcast

## The Situation

You just accepted a new PM role. Maybe it is your first PM job, or maybe you are joining a new company after years in the discipline. Either way, you are walking into a room full of people who already have context, relationships, and opinions -- and you have none of these things yet. The stakes feel enormous because first impressions compound. The decisions you make in your first 90 days will determine whether you are seen as a credible partner or an outsider who never quite earned a seat at the table.

What makes this moment so difficult is the paradox at its center: you need to build credibility before you can drive change, but you were hired to drive change. Move too fast and you alienate your team. Move too slowly and people wonder why you were hired. The best PMs navigate this tension by being intensely curious in the early weeks, earning trust through small wins, and only then asserting a point of view on the bigger questions.

The emotional texture of this period is also underappreciated. Impostor syndrome peaks in the first 90 days. You will sit in meetings where acronyms fly past you, where team dynamics you do not yet understand shape every interaction, and where the temptation to pretend you know more than you do is powerful. Resisting that temptation -- and instead leading with questions -- is the single most important thing you can do.

## The Playbook

### Phase 1: Listen and Learn (Week 1-3)

1. **Schedule 1:1s with every key stakeholder.** Engineering leads, designers, data scientists, your manager, peer PMs, and the most tenured IC on your team. Ask each person: What is working? What is broken? What do you wish the PM on this team would do differently?
2. **Map the product's current state.** Use the product yourself obsessively. File bugs. Go through every user flow. Read the last three months of user feedback, support tickets, and NPS comments.
3. **Understand the metrics.** Get access to dashboards. Learn which metrics the team tracks, which ones leadership cares about, and where the gaps are between the two.
4. **Study the strategy documents.** Read the most recent roadmap, strategy doc, OKRs, and any post-mortems. Understand the reasoning behind recent decisions, not just the decisions themselves.
5. **Build the relationship triad.** As Lenny advises, the PM-EM-Design Manager triad is the foundation of a high-functioning team. Invest heavily in these two relationships from day one. Sit together, set up a weekly leads sync, and begin building rapport outside of work contexts.
6. **Resist the urge to propose changes.** Your only job in this phase is to absorb context and build trust through active listening.

### Phase 2: Earn Quick Wins (Week 3-6)

1. **Identify one small, high-confidence improvement and ship it.** This could be a bug fix, a UX improvement the team has been wanting, or an unblocking decision that has been stuck. The goal is to demonstrate that you can execute.
2. **Create a source-of-truth document.** Lenny's three-step framework for solving problems starts with crystallizing the problem. Write up the current team mission, top priorities, key metrics, and open questions in a single document. Share it for feedback. This positions you as someone who creates clarity.
3. **Start speaking with customers.** Set up at least five customer conversations. As Lenny emphasizes in his 14 habits, great PMs speak with customers regularly. This also gives you insights no one else on the team has, which is how you start adding unique value.
4. **Begin forming your own point of view.** Based on everything you have learned, start developing hypotheses about what should change. Share these tentatively with your manager and triad partners, using the template Lenny cites from Jackie Bavaro: "Here is a challenge I noticed, here is how I am thinking about it, any thoughts?"
5. **Unblock something for your team.** Lenny's habit #7: great PMs endlessly look for blockers to unblock. Find a decision that has been stuck, a dependency that needs escalation, or a process that is slowing people down, and resolve it.

### Phase 3: Assert Your Vision (Week 6-12)

1. **Draft a 90-day strategy document.** Synthesize your learnings into a clear assessment of where the product is, where it should go, and what the team should focus on next. Include mission, vision, strategic bets, and a proposed roadmap.
2. **Present your strategy to stakeholders.** Get feedback, iterate, and align. Use Jules Walter's influence tactics: seek intel on how each stakeholder makes decisions, frame your message from their POV, and prime key people before the meeting.
3. **Set or refine team goals.** Propose specific, measurable goals tied to the strategy. Make them ambitious but believable.
4. **Establish your operating cadence.** Weekly team syncs, regular design reviews, stakeholder updates, and a planning rhythm. This is how you institutionalize alignment.
5. **Have a candid conversation with your manager.** Share what you have learned, where you see the biggest opportunities, and ask for explicit feedback on how you are doing. Get aligned on expectations for the next quarter.

## Key Insights from Lenny's Archive

1. **"Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become."** Lenny opens his 14 habits piece with this James Clear quote, emphasizing that PM excellence is built through daily habits, not grand gestures. (Source: `14-habits-of-highly-effective-product-managers.md`)

2. **"Great PMs build an aura of 'I've got this.'"** They rarely drop balls, they come prepared, and their colleagues know that when they take on a task, it will get done. Be very selective with what you take on, and spend more time than anyone else on the things you do. (Source: `14-habits-of-highly-effective-product-managers.md`)

3. **"Most disagreements are the result of having different information."** When you find your team heading in the wrong direction, come back to the facts. Assume you are wrong. This is especially important when you are new and do not yet have full context. (Source: `autonomy-vs-direction---issue-35.md`)

4. **"The three most important differentiators of senior PMs are strategy, autonomy, and nuance."** Jackie Bavaro's framework shows that the fastest way to earn trust is to demonstrate strategic thinking, the ability to operate independently, and comfort with ambiguity. (Source: `becoming-a-senior-product-manager.md`)

5. **"Nailing the problem statement is the single most important step in solving any problem."** Before proposing solutions, make sure your team is aligned on the problem. Problem statements are like silver burritos -- everyone on the team has a unique version in their heads. (Source: `a-three-step-framework-for-solving-problems.md`)

6. **The "dinosaur brain" principle from Ami Vora:** Executives have limited bandwidth. When presenting to leadership, do not dump information -- make a recommendation. "My manager owns context, I own the recommendation." This applies from day one. (Source: `ami-vora.md`)

7. **Fareed Mosavat on building trust:** "You can't do homework. You can't do exercises. You have to work on real products at real companies with real customers to get better at product management." The real acceleration happens from doing, not just observing. (Source: `fareed-mosavat.md`)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Proposing a new strategy in week one.** You do not have enough context yet. Every experienced PM who was interviewed on Lenny's podcast emphasizes the importance of listening before acting.

2. **Trying to prove you were worth hiring by shipping something big immediately.** This often leads to poorly-scoped projects that damage credibility rather than build it.

3. **Ignoring the existing team culture and norms.** Every team has unwritten rules. Violating them early -- even with good intentions -- creates friction that takes months to repair.

4. **Failing to build the PM-EM-Design triad.** Lenny calls this out as habit #8. If you skip this, your team will operate as three separate functions rather than one aligned unit.

5. **Not talking to customers early enough.** It is easy to get consumed by internal meetings and stakeholder management. But direct customer insight is your fastest path to adding unique value and forming a credible point of view.

## Signs You're on Track

1. Your engineering lead and design partner are proactively sharing context with you and seeking your input on decisions.
2. You can articulate the team's top three priorities and the reasoning behind them, and your manager agrees with your assessment.
3. You have shipped at least one small win that the team appreciates.
4. Stakeholders are starting to come to you with questions and context, rather than routing around you.
5. You have a written strategy document that has been reviewed and endorsed by your triad and manager.

## Signs You're Off Track

1. After six weeks, you still cannot explain the team's strategy or key metrics without looking them up.
2. Your engineering and design partners are making decisions without involving you or informing you after the fact.
3. You have not spoken to a single customer.
4. Your manager is giving you increasingly specific direction rather than trusting you to figure things out.
5. Team members are visibly skeptical when you share opinions or propose ideas in meetings.
